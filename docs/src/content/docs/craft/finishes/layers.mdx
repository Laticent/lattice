A finish is a stack of up to four layers. Each has a job, a slot name, and
a fixed place in the pile.

| Layer | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Wash**<br/>`--fin-wash` | an ambient field of color | corner glow, spotlight, mesh, duotone |
| **2 · Texture**<br/>`--fin-texture` | a repeating pattern | grid, dots, hatch, ruled, pinstripe, lattice |
| **3 · Mark**<br/>`--fin-mark` | a placed emblem | monogram, rings, a ghost numeral |
| **4 · Edge**<br/>`--fin-edge` | the boundary | vignette, margin rule, fold, keyline frame |

Content sits above all four, always.

You do not need all of them. Most good finishes use two. `atrium`, the
quietest one that ships, is a corner glow plus a dissolving grid plus a
left margin rule — and you would struggle to describe it after seeing it,
which is the compliment.

## 1 · Wash — the color field

A gradient of the accent, fading out. It gives the page a direction: light
falling from a corner, a soft pool behind the title.

```css
--fin-wash:
  radial-gradient(ellipse 120% 95% at 100% 0%,
    color-mix(in srgb, var(--accent) 14%, transparent) 0%,
    transparent 60%);
```

Two things carry the whole effect. **The alpha** — 14% here — decides
whether this is atmosphere or a stain. Stay between about 5% and 16%. **The
position** — `at 100% 0%` — puts it in the top-right corner; move it and
the light moves.

## 2 · Texture — the pattern

Repeating lines, dots or hatching. Where a wash gives direction, a texture
gives the page a surface — the difference between paper and glass.

```css
--fin-texture:
  repeating-linear-gradient(0deg,
    color-mix(in srgb, var(--text-heading) 6%, transparent) 0 1px,
    transparent 1px 34px);
```

Read that as: a 1-pixel line, then 33 pixels of nothing, forever. Change
`0deg` to `90deg` for verticals; declare both for a grid. Textures mix
toward the ink color rather than the accent, because a colored pattern
reads as decoration while a gray one reads as paper.

Keep the alpha even lower than a wash — 4% to 8%. A texture covers the
whole page, so it accumulates.

## Stacking two layers

Here is where the one real piece of bookkeeping lives.

The wash and the texture share one CSS `background-image`, so they are one
list, and **texture is listed first**. Three companion properties describe
that list — size, position, repeat — and each needs **one entry per layer,
in the same order**:

```css
--fin-size:     auto,   auto;
--fin-position: center, 0% 100%;
--fin-repeat:   repeat, no-repeat;
/*              ^texture ^wash */
```

Get the counts out of step and the layers take each other's settings —
which usually looks like a texture that has stopped repeating.

## 3 · Mark — the emblem

A single placed glyph: a monogram in the corner, a ghost numeral behind the
heading, a small rule.

```css
--fin-mark-text: "";
```

**Default it to empty**, always. A deck-wide finish with `"ACME"` baked in
is a finish only one organization can use. Leave it blank and let the
author fill it in per slide when they want it:

```css
section.finish-myfinish { --fin-mark-text: "Q3"; }
```

## 4 · Edge — the boundary

What happens at the rim of the page: a vignette darkening the corners, a
rule down the left margin, a keyline frame all the way around.

A full frame is drawn with stacked inset shadows through `--fin-frame`, not
with a border and not with the section's own `::after` — that one is
reserved for the page number, and a frame drawn there erases it.

## Composing them

A finish worth shipping has **a point of view** — one signature layer that
makes it recognizable. `loom` weaves a lattice at 45 degrees. `savile` is a
tailored pinstripe. `gallery` hangs a museum keyline.

A wash of the accent, on its own, is not a finish. It is what every deck
would look like if nobody had decided anything.
