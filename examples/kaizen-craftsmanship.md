# The Invisible Half

`Craft · Quality · Kaizen`

Most of what decides whether a thing is good is hidden from the person who buys it. Today we go looking for it.

<!-- Open cold. The two pairs are on the table, face down, and nobody touches them
     until the next slide. Do not name the brands, the prices, or which is which.
     Hold two seconds after the subtitle before you move on. -->

---

<!-- _class: list-steps -->

`Do this first · 8 minutes`

## Handle both pairs before anyone says a word about them.

1. Handle, don't judge
   - Pick each one up. Flex the sole. Look inside. Say nothing to anyone yet.
2. Write ten differences
   - In your own words. No technical vocabulary is required, and none of it would help you here.
3. Circle the buyer's three
   - Which would someone catch in ninety seconds, in a shop, with no tools and nobody to ask?
4. Name what you can't check
   - One difference you suspect but cannot prove by looking. This is the one that matters.

<!-- Set a real timer. Silence is the whole point — the moment they talk, the
     loudest person's categories become everyone's categories, and you lose the
     independent observations you need for the debrief.

     Step 4 is the hinge of the session. Most groups produce nothing for it on the
     first pass. That blank is the lesson: they have no vocabulary for the part
     that matters, because nobody ever showed it to them. Don't rescue them. -->

<!-- _class: compare-prose -->

`Debrief · What your list says`

## Nearly everything you wrote down lives on the outside.

- What the eye catches
  - Stitch spacing, edge finish, how the leather creases at the vamp. Real signals, every one of them cheap to imitate.
- What the eye cannot
  - How the upper was tensioned over the last. What the sole is actually attached to. Whether it can ever be repaired.

> The visible half sells the object. The invisible half decides how long you keep it.

<!-- Collect the left column from the room out loud, on a whiteboard, in their
     words. Then read the right column yourself and watch it land.

     The trap to name: every item on the left can be faked cheaply. Machine
     stitching can be tightened to look hand-close. Edges can be burnished on a
     shoe that will fall apart. The surface is the easiest thing to buy. -->

<!-- _class: content -->

`The reveal`

## The part that decides its lifespan is a ridge you will never see.

On the good pair a rib — the holdfast — is carved from the insole itself, and the sole is stitched through it. On the other, glued canvas imitates that rib. From every angle a buyer reaches, they are identical. One resoles a dozen times. The other lets go when the glue tires.

> Every craft has a holdfast — the part nobody inspects, that decides everything.

<!-- If you have a cutaway or a failed pair, this is the moment to pass it around.

     Push the generalization hard, because this is the sentence the session exists
     to deliver: every discipline has a holdfast. In code it's the test suite and
     the error paths. In a service it's the handoff nobody owns. In a document
     it's the reasoning behind the recommendation. Nobody inspects any of them,
     and all of them decide how long the work survives contact with reality.

     Ask the room for their own holdfast before you move on. -->

> Inspection does not improve the quality, nor guarantee quality. Inspection is too late. The quality, good or bad, is already in the product.

— W. Edwards Deming, *Out of the Crisis*, 1982

<!-- Land it, then stop talking. Let them read it twice.

<!-- _class: split-panel -->

`Deming · The system`

## Nobody in that workshop set out to make a bad shoe.

Paid by the pair and measured on pairs, the maker did the rational thing.

- A bad system beats a good person
  - Deming put 94% of trouble in the system, 6% in people.
- A quota buys the number you named
  - Pay by the pair and you specified pairs, not shoes.
- Fear hides defects, never removes them
  - Punish the messenger and reports stop. The defects continue.

<!-- This is the slide that changes how they manage people, so slow down.

     The instinct in the room will be to blame the maker. Name that instinct out
     loud, then take it apart: a skilled person, paid by the pair and watched on
     pairs, will find the fastest invisible saving available. That is not a
     character flaw, it is arithmetic.

     94/6 is Deming's own revision of his earlier 85/15 estimate, and his exact
     words are worth having: "I should estimate that in my experience most
     troubles and most possibilities for improvement add up to the proportions
     something like this: 94% belongs to the system (responsibility of
     management), 6% special." Out of the Crisis, p. 315. Note "I should
     estimate" — he is offering judgment, not a measurement, so don't defend the
     figure. Defend the direction.

     Then ask the room: what does your team get paid and measured on, and what
     invisible saving does that make rational? -->

<!-- _class: stats -->

`1-10-100 · Cost of poor quality`

## The same defect costs ten times more at every handoff it survives.

`A rule of thumb: the multiplier is approximate, the direction is not.`

1. $1
   - caught at design
2. $10
   - caught in production
3. $100
   - caught by the customer

— None of it appears on a budget line. Rework is done by people already on payroll.

<!-- Land the note aloud, because it is the whole slide: the cost is real, it is
     large, and it is invisible. It never shows up as a cost. It shows up as
     "we're busy." That is the hidden factory — a second production line running
     inside the first, staffed by people already on payroll, making nothing.

     Then make it theirs. Ask them to take their own last defect and price it in
     all three columns. Most will find they only ever paid attention at $100, and
     that the $1 fix was available and boring.

     ON THE NUMBERS, if anyone presses — and let them press, this is a quality
     session. The 1-10-100 ratio is a rule of thumb, not a measurement, which is
     why the slide says so. The often-quoted "cost of poor quality is 15-25% of
     revenue" is attributed to Juran everywhere and sourced to a primary text
     almost nowhere; Crosby claimed 20-25% of revenue, but that was his own
     estimate about his own consulting market. Do not lend those figures more
     authority than they have. The escalation is well evidenced and the direction
     is not in dispute; the exact multiplier is a teaching device. Saying that out
     loud is not a weakness in the argument. It is the argument. -->

<!-- _class: cycle -->

`Kaizen · The loop`

## Improvement is a loop you run, not a project you finish.

- Plan
  - State what you expect, and why. A prediction, not a task.
- Do
  - Try it small. One bench, one batch, one week.
- Study
  - Compare what happened against what you predicted, not against hope.
- Act
  - Make it the new standard, or drop it and plan again.

<!-- Deming fought for "Study" over "Check" for the rest of his life, and the
     distinction is the whole loop. Check asks "did we do it?" Study asks "was our
     theory right?" Only the second one teaches you anything.

     The prediction in Plan is what makes Study possible. A change with no
     prediction attached cannot be wrong, which means it cannot be learned from. -->

<!-- _class: cards-grid four -->

`Kaizen · In practice`

## Four habits turn improvement from an event into a rhythm.

- Go and see
  - Decisions belong at the bench, not in a summary of the bench. Walk to where the work happens.
- Standard first
  - You cannot improve what was never written down. An unwritten process that changes has not improved — it has drifted.
- Mistake-proof it
  - Change the tool so the error becomes impossible. Asking people to be more careful is not a countermeasure.
- Stop the line
  - Anyone may halt work on a defect. Passing it downstream turns a cheap problem into an expensive one.

<!-- Land this line yourself, since it isn't on the slide: kaizen is not a program
     you launch, it is what you do between programs.

     The Japanese names, if you want them: genchi genbutsu (go to the actual
     place), poka-yoke (mistake-proofing), jidoka and the andon cord (stop the
     line). Say them once, then go back to the English — the habit matters, the
     vocabulary doesn't.

     "Standard first" is the one they'll skip. Push it: an improvement to an
     unwritten process is indistinguishable from that process drifting, and next
     month nobody can tell which happened. -->

<!-- _class: compare-prose chosen -->

`Modularity · The welt`

## A welted shoe is not one object; it is two, joined by a standard.

- Glued construction
  - Sole and upper are one object. When the cheapest part wears out the whole shoe is scrap, because there is nothing to take apart.
- Welted construction
  - A standard joint sits between them. The sole becomes a consumable, and the shoe outlives ten of them.

> The welt is boring, invisible, and the most consequential decision in the shoe.

<!-- Point at the welt on the good pair while this is up, and trace it with a
     finger all the way round. It is a strip of leather most people would call a
     decorative edge.

     The generalization: an interface is what converts a permanent decision into
     a reversible one. The welt does not make the sole better — it makes the sole
     REPLACEABLE, which is what lets it get better later.

     Then ask the question and wait for answers: what in your work is glued that
     should be welted? -->

`Modularity · A second specimen`

## This badge is hand-stitched, and designed to come apart.

- Two bodies
  - A card holder with a window, and a flap pouch that shuts with a snap. Either one, same attachment.
- Three carries
  - Neck lanyard, belt clip, key ring. Each terminates the same way, so any carry takes any body.
- One interface
  - A snapped leather tab and a solid brass clip. That single agreement is what the system is built on.

> Five pieces, six configurations. Without the shared tab, that is six separate products.

<!-- Take it apart in front of them, then hand the pieces round and make them
     rebuild it a different way. The swap is the argument; describing it is not.

     Three things to point at, in this order. First the saddle stitch — the same
     stitch, in the same veg-tan, by the same hand as the outsole they were
     holding an hour ago. This is not an analogy for the shoe. It is the same
     craft. Second, the leather has darkened where it gets handled: quality that
     only shows itself after you own it, which is the holdfast argument again.

     Third, the hardware is SOLID BRASS, not plated. Say it and then ask how they
     would tell. They can't — plated hardware is identical on day one, and gives
     itself away years later when the plating wears through at the wear point and
     the base metal underneath starts to rust. Solid brass has no layer to lose;
     it just goes darker. That is a holdfast: invisible at purchase, decisive at
     year five. Worth admitting I got this wrong myself from a photograph — I
     called it nickel, off the surface, at a glance, which is the exact error
     slide three is about.

     Then the design point: the hard work was never the carries. It was settling
     the tab once, correctly, so that every carry after it could be easy. Get a
     carry wrong and you have lost one carry. Get the tab wrong and you have lost
     all six configurations, permanently. -->

<!-- _class: content -->

`The synthesis`

## Modularity is what makes continuous improvement affordable.

Improvement costs whatever it costs to change one thing. In a glued shoe the sole *is* the shoe, so a better sole means a whole new shoe and nothing improves. Put a standard joint between them and the sole improves every year while the rest stays untouched. The same holds for your code, your process, your handoffs.

> Kaizen without modularity is good intentions with a large bill attached.

<!-- This is the sentence to leave them with, so deliver it slowly.

     Everyone agrees with continuous improvement in the abstract. The reason it
     dies in practice is almost never willpower — it's that in a tightly coupled
     system the smallest worthwhile change is enormous, so nobody makes it. The
     work that makes kaizen possible is boundary work, done before the improvement
     anyone can see. -->

`Your practice · This week`

## Two things you can start on Monday without asking permission.

1. Find the holdfast in your work — the part nobody inspects, that decides how long it lasts.
2. When something goes wrong, fix the system that made it rational before you talk to the person.
